"Clicks used to be cheaper" is one of the most common phrases in conversations about Google Ads. Sometimes it is true; sometimes it is the result of settings that can be fixed. To tell the difference, you need to know how the auction works: why one ad appears above another, and why different advertisers pay different amounts for the same query.

Below we go through the auction mechanics as described in Google's own help documentation, the reasons cost per click rises, and what to look at in your reports if advertising in Kazakhstan has become more expensive.

## How the auction works

Every time someone searches on Google, an auction runs among the advertisers whose keywords match the query. The auction decides two things: whether an ad is shown at all, and in which position.

The decision is based on **Ad Rank**. According to Google Ads Help, it takes into account:

- **your bid** — how much you are willing to pay for a click;
- **auction-time ad quality** — expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience;
- **Ad Rank thresholds** — the minimum quality an ad must reach to appear in a given position;
- **the competitiveness of the auction** — if two ads have similar Ad Rank, they have similar chances at a position;
- **the context of the search** — the query itself, the person's location and device, the time, other ads and results on the page;
- **the expected impact of assets** — extra elements of the ad such as sitelinks, callouts, phone number and address.

The key takeaway: you cannot simply buy a position with a higher bid. A high-quality ad can rank above a competitor with a lower bid, while a weak ad may not show at all, even with a high bid, if it does not clear the thresholds.

## What you actually pay per click

Your maximum bid is a ceiling, not a price. As Google puts it, you are often charged less — sometimes much less — than your maximum CPC: you pay what is needed to clear the Ad Rank thresholds and beat the advertiser ranked immediately below you.

The practical consequence: improving ad quality and landing pages lowers cost per click just as really as lowering bids — without losing position.

## Quality Score is a diagnostic, not a lever

In Google Ads, keywords have a **Quality Score** from 1 to 10 and three components: expected CTR, ad relevance and landing page experience. Google is explicit that Quality Score is not an input in the ad auction; it is a diagnostic tool that helps you find weak spots.

How to read it:

- **below-average expected CTR** — the ad does not grab attention: generic text, nothing specific from the query, a weaker offer than competitors';
- **below-average ad relevance** — the ad group mixes queries that are too different, and the ad answers none of them precisely;
- **below-average landing page experience** — the page does not deliver what the ad promises, loads slowly or is awkward on a phone.

## Why cost per click goes up

The reasons split into external ones you cannot control and internal ones you can fix.

### External reasons

- **More advertisers in the auction.** New players enter the niche, or large players increase budgets.
- **Seasonality.** Competition for the same queries rises in peak season.
- **Market changes.** Marketplaces and aggregators start advertising on the same queries.

### Internal reasons

- **Broad keywords without negative keywords.** Ads show for irrelevant queries, CTR drops, quality falls and clicks get more expensive.
- **Mixed ad groups.** Different services in one group, so no ad matches any query precisely.
- **A weak landing page.** The home page instead of a page for the query, slow loading, an awkward form.
- **Automated bidding without data.** Bid strategies that optimise for conversions learn from conversion data. If conversions are not set up or are polluted (for example, every button click counts), the algorithm learns the wrong thing.
- **Constraints that are too tight.** A very small geography or schedule combined with ambitious targets can force the system to fight over a handful of impressions.

## Automated bidding: when to switch it on

Google Ads offers bid strategies that set a bid for each auction automatically: Maximise conversions, Target CPA, Target ROAS, Maximise conversion value. They use signals you cannot use manually: device, time, location, user behaviour.

But automation has a precondition: it learns from conversions. So the order is:

1. **First, accurate conversion tracking.** Count real enquiries: submitted forms, calls of a meaningful length, clicks to a messenger. Counting every button click pollutes the data.
2. **Then, data accumulation.** The algorithm needs a certain volume of conversions to draw conclusions. At the start, advertisers often use a strategy that maximises clicks or conversions without strict targets.
3. **Then, targets.** Set the target CPA from actual data, not from a wished-for number. An overly ambitious target sharply restricts delivery.
4. **Best of all — importing sales from the CRM.** If Google Ads learns which enquiries became deals, the algorithm optimises for customers rather than any enquiry.

Sharp changes to targets and budgets send a strategy back into learning. Change them gradually and judge the result after a week or two, not sooner.

## What is specific to Kazakhstan

- **Two search languages.** People search in Russian and Kazakh, sometimes mixing them or using transliteration. Kazakh-language queries are a separate keyword set, often with less competition. They need ads in Kazakh and, where possible, a page in the same language.
- **Cities and districts.** Demand and competition differ between Almaty, Astana and Shymkent. It makes sense to split campaigns, or at least analyse results by city.
- **Messengers as conversions.** A large share of enquiries comes via WhatsApp. If clicks on the messenger button are not tracked as conversions, campaigns look worse than they perform, and automated strategies are starved of data.
- **Not only Google.** Part of search demand in Kazakhstan goes through other search engines too, so when planning, look at search as a whole, not just Google.

## A campaign structure that saves money

1. **Separate campaigns for different goals:** search for high-intent queries, search for informational queries, remarketing and, where it makes sense, Performance Max.
2. **Ad groups built around one theme.** One service or one query type per group, with ads that use the wording of those queries.
3. **Negative keywords from day one**, and regular work with the search terms report.
4. **Deliberate match types.** Broad match works alongside automated bidding and good conversion data; with weak data, phrase and exact match are safer.
5. **Every ad asset filled in:** callouts, sitelinks, phone, address. They are part of Ad Rank.

## What to look at in reports

- **Search terms report** — the actual queries your ads showed for. The main source of negative keywords and new ideas.
- **Quality Score and its components** — where the problem is: the ad, the group or the page.
- **Impression share and lost impression share** — how many impressions you lose to budget and how many to rank. Losses to rank are fixed with quality, not money.
- **Auction insights** — who competes with you and how often they rank above you.
- **Cost per conversion and its quality** — not just what an enquiry costs, but how many enquiries become sales according to the CRM.

## A check before raising the budget

If cost per click has gone up, run through this list before adding budget:

- which queries you are showing for now, and how many are irrelevant;
- whether the ads in each group match its queries;
- whether each group leads to a page built for its query;
- whether conversions are counted correctly, including calls and messengers;
- how many impressions are lost to rank and how many to budget;
- how the cost per customer — not per click — has changed.

It often turns out that cost per click went up while cost per customer did not — or the reverse: clicks are cheap and there are no sales.
