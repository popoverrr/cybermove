The most expensive mistake in marketing is increasing the ad budget when the problem is not the ads. Traffic amplifies whatever is already there: if the offer is weak, the website loses leads and the sales team replies half a day later, more ad spend simply means bigger losses. A business audit exists to find out, before you launch or scale, exactly where the money leaks and what to fix first.

Below is how we look at a business before advertising: the symptoms that say an audit is needed, the six areas we check, what you should receive at the end, and how to run a first self-diagnosis on your own.

## Symptoms: when the budget is being wasted

Nobody calls with "we need an audit". The problem usually sounds like this:

- ads bring in leads but few sales, and nobody can say whether the traffic or the sales team is to blame;
- the ad agency reports great clicks and cheap leads, yet revenue does not grow;
- every new channel "doesn't work", so the team keeps switching platforms;
- the owner does not know what one customer costs or how much a customer brings over time;
- revenue grows, but there is no more cash in the bank.

What these situations share is that decisions are made on gut feeling or on intermediate metrics — clicks, reach, cost per lead — rather than on money. An audit turns the conversation back to money.

## Six areas to check

### 1. Product and offer

We start with what you sell, not with the ads. Who buys today, and why? Where does the offer lose to competitors — on price, promise, terms or delivery time? Does a customer have a clear reason to choose you over the next result on the page? If the offer is not defined, advertising has to sell "everything to everyone", and the cost of a customer goes up.

### 2. Positioning

How the market sees you and how you describe yourself. Companies often present themselves as a list of services, while customers are looking for a solution to a specific problem. We review the website, social media, sales proposals and ads: do they send one message, and does it match what customers actually pay you for? How to build that message is covered in our guide to [company positioning](/en/insights/brand-content/positioning-one-sentence/).

### 3. Channels and funnel

Where leads come from, what each one costs and what share reaches payment — for each channel separately. Averages hide the truth: one channel may bring half the revenue on a small share of the budget, another the opposite. We map the customer journey step by step — first touch, enquiry, reply, meeting or quote, payment, repeat purchase — and measure conversion at each step.

### 4. Sales team

The most common source of losses, and the one you cannot see from an ad account. We look at first-response time, missed calls and unanswered messages, scripts, objection handling and CRM discipline. We listen to call recordings and read chats, because that is where you see why an enquiry never became a deal. Sometimes one rule — such as replying to every lead within a working hour — does more than doubling the budget.

### 5. Finance and unit economics

What it costs to acquire a customer (CAC), how much they bring over their lifetime (LTV), what margin remains after direct costs, and whether each channel pays back (ROMI). Without these numbers you cannot say how much you can afford to spend on ads. We explain how to calculate them without complicated spreadsheets in a separate guide on [unit economics](/en/insights/audit/unit-economics-cac-ltv-romi/).

### 6. Systems and data

Are leads from every channel visible in one place? Are the website, phone system, messengers and CRM connected? Is there end-to-end analytics, or are reports assembled by hand? If the data is scattered, any conclusion about advertising is a guess. We also check the basics: do forms work, do notifications arrive, are messenger enquiries being lost?

## What you should get at the end

A good audit does not end with a presentation full of generic advice. It ends with documents you can make decisions from:

- **A diagnosis for every funnel step** — where the business loses money and how much, in currency rather than impressions.
- **A map of problems and opportunities** — every finding with an estimate of impact and cost.
- **Priorities** — what to fix in the first weeks, what to plan for the quarter, what to leave alone.
- **A list of metrics** to track after the changes, with their baseline values so there is something to compare against.

If needed, the audit continues into a [strategy and roadmap](/en/services/audit/strategy/): a 3–12 month plan with a budget, owners and KPIs. But even on its own, the map of problems is a complete result your team can work from.

## Self-diagnosis checklist

Before ordering an audit, it helps to answer a few questions honestly. If you cannot answer half of them, it is too early to scale advertising.

1. Can you name the three main reasons customers choose you — in their words, not yours?
2. Do you know the cost per lead and the cost per customer for each channel last quarter?
3. Do you know what share of leads reaches payment, and where the rest are lost?
4. How long does it take from an enquiry to the first reply? Has anyone measured it?
5. Do all enquiries — website forms, messengers, calls — land in one system?
6. Do you know how much an average customer brings over the whole relationship?
7. Can you say which channels pay back and which do not, and based on what data?
8. Does cash in the bank grow together with revenue, or do you hit cash gaps?

Honest "I don't know" answers are not a reason to panic. They are exactly where an audit starts.

## Typical findings

Every business is different, but some findings come up again and again. If you recognise yourself here, that is good news: these problems are usually fixed without increasing the budget.

- **The ad promises one thing, the page shows another.** Someone clicks on a specific offer and lands on a home page listing every service. Many leave immediately, and the ads look expensive when the real problem is the landing page.
- **Messenger enquiries live separately.** WhatsApp or Telegram messages are handled on sales reps' personal phones and never reach the CRM. They are missing from reports, the cost per customer is miscalculated, and some enquiries disappear without a trace.
- **The cheapest channel per lead is the most expensive per customer.** Cheap leads often mean the wrong audience. As long as only cost per lead is measured, budget flows to where sales are lowest.
- **Discounts eat the margin.** Promotions lift revenue while profit stays flat or falls. Without margin by product, you will not see this in the ad account or the CRM.
- **Nobody works on repeat sales.** All the attention goes to new customers, although acquisition usually pays back through repeat purchases. Reminders, loyalty offers and working the customer base often bring quick results.
- **The agency report and the CRM disagree.** The ad account shows one number of conversions and the CRM another, and nobody reconciles them. Decisions are made on whichever number looks better.

## How to tell a real audit from a formality

It is easy to turn an audit into a nice document nobody reads. A few signs the work was done properly:

1. **Conclusions rely on your data.** If the recommendations would fit any company in your industry, it is a template, not an audit.
2. **Losses are measured in money.** "Low website conversion" is an observation; "this step loses this share of leads, and here is what it costs per month" is a diagnosis.
3. **There are priorities.** A list of fifty equally important points does not help. You need three to five first steps with a clear expected effect.
4. **It says what not to do.** A good audit removes tasks as well as adding them: channels that do not pay back, reports nobody reads.
5. **Baselines are recorded.** Without them, three months later you cannot tell whether the changes worked.

## From our practice

In projects with operational management, such as the [barbershop chain in Kyiv](/en/cases/barbershops-kyiv/), growth is limited not by advertising but by processes: barber utilisation, booking, repeat visits and service standards. In large marketing projects, such as the [BRSM-NAFTA fuel station network](/en/cases/brsm-nafta/), consistency matters most: campaigns, brand communication and points of sale must say the same thing. In both cases the first step is the same — understand where the business is losing money today.

## Where to start

The minimum data for an initial review: an export of leads and deals for recent months (from a CRM or spreadsheets), read access to ad accounts and analytics, and an idea of the margin on your main products. That is enough to spot the most visible losses in a single meeting and decide whether you need a full [sales and marketing audit](/en/services/audit/business-audit/).

The main rule: diagnosis first, budget second. Advertising is an amplifier, and you want it to amplify what makes money.
