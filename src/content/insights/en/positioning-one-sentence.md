Ask five people at the same company what makes it different from competitors. You will probably hear five different answers, and most of them will sound like everyone else's: "quality", "a personal approach", "a team of professionals", "affordable prices". Customers hear the same thing from every company they consider — and end up choosing on price.

Positioning is a decision about the place you occupy in the mind of a specific customer. It is not an advertising line or a mission statement on the office wall; it is a working tool from which the website, advertising, sales scripts and content grow. A good test: can you state it in one sentence that a competitor could not repeat without lying? Below is a method for building that sentence, illustrative examples and ways to check whether it works.

## Why "quality and a personal approach" doesn't work

These words are not wrong — they are useless. They cannot be verified before a purchase, and everyone says them. If you remove your company name from your description and the text would fit any competitor, that is not positioning, it is filler.

Weak positioning has recognisable signs:

- the description starts with a list of services rather than the customer's problem;
- the target audience is "small and medium businesses" or "anyone who needs it";
- differences are expressed through adjectives: best, reliable, modern;
- there is no proof, or the proof is unrelated to the promise.

## The method: segment → problem → difference → proof

The formula we use when working on a brand core has four parts. Each has to be filled in concretely and then combined into one sentence.

### 1. Segment: who exactly

Not "everyone", but a group of customers with a similar situation and a similar reason to buy. A segment can be defined by industry, size, stage of development or trigger event: "chains with three or more locations", "manufacturers entering public tenders", "experts launching their first course".

The fear of narrowing the segment is understandable: it feels like turning other customers away. In practice, narrow positioning does not stop you selling to others — it helps you convince faster the people you suit best.

### 2. Problem: what hurts

The problem is phrased in the customer's words, not yours. Not "insufficient effectiveness of marketing communications" but "we run ads and get few enquiries" or "every new sales rep leaves after three months". The best source of wording is call recordings, chats and customer interviews.

### 3. Difference: what you do differently

This is the answer to "why you and not the others", and it has to be verifiable. The difference can lie in the product, the way you work, speed, guarantees, specialisation, industry experience or pricing model. The key test: a competitor cannot say the same thing without lying.

### 4. Proof: why it is believable

Case studies, real numbers, certificates, guarantees written into the contract, well-known clients, an open methodology. The proof should support the difference specifically, not the company in general.

## Where to find the material: customer interviews

The most accurate wording for positioning lives not in the team's heads but in customers' words. Short interviews — five to ten conversations of twenty to thirty minutes — give more than long internal debates.

Who to invite:

- recent customers who chose you — they remember how they made the decision;
- loyal long-term customers — they know what they value you for;
- people who considered you but chose a competitor — they will be the most honest about your weak points.

What to ask:

1. What problem made you start looking for a solution? What was happening at the time?
2. What other options did you consider?
3. Why did you choose us (or not)? What was decisive?
4. What made you hesitate before buying?
5. How would you describe us to a friend who needs the same thing?

Write the answers down word for word. Repeated words and phrases are ready-made material for the segment, the problem and the difference. The last question is especially useful: customers often phrase your positioning better than you do.

## Putting the sentence together

Template: **"For [segment] who [problem], we [difference] — [proof]."** In the final version the template disappears and a natural sentence remains.

Below are **illustrative examples** for fictional companies, to show the difference between "before" and "after".

**Boiler maintenance service.**
Before: "Quality maintenance of heating equipment by professionals."
After: "We service boilers in private homes before the heating season, so you never have to hunt for a technician in the frost: a scheduled visit, cleaning and inspection with a written report on the system's condition."

**Accounting firm.**
Before: "A full range of accounting services for business."
After: "We keep the books for online stores selling on marketplaces, reconciling platform payouts with sales so you know the real margin on every product."

**English school for adults.**
Before: "English for all levels with experienced teachers."
After: "English for professionals with a job interview in English in six months: lessons built on the material of your profession and a mock interview every month."

In all three "after" versions the segment and the problem are recognisable at once, and the difference can be checked.

## Positioning and price

Weak positioning almost always ends in haggling. If the customer sees no difference between you and competitors, price is the only thing left to compare. Strong positioning changes the question: the customer compares not "who is cheaper" but "who will solve my problem better".

That does not mean you can raise prices the day after writing a new statement. But a company that clearly explains its difference and backs it up has a reason to stay out of price wars and to work with the customers for whom that difference matters.

## How to test the statement

1. **The swap test.** Replace your company name with a competitor's. If the sentence is still true, there is no difference.
2. **The customer test.** Read the sentence to three to five real customers and ask: "Is this about you? What is the main point here?" If they retell the same thing in their own words, it works.
3. **The sales test.** Ask reps to use the new statement in first conversations for a week and note the reactions. Fewer "so how are you different?" questions is a good sign.
4. **The ad test.** Compare ads with the old and new promise on the same budget. Look not only at clicks but at the quality of the enquiries.

## What changes after positioning

Positioning is not a paragraph on the "About" page. Once it is agreed, a lot changes:

- **the website** opens with the segment's problem, not a list of services;
- **advertising** gets a clear promise and a narrow audience, and so finds the right people more cheaply;
- **sales** get an answer to "why you" and arguments for typical objections;
- **content** stops being "posts on schedule" and answers the chosen segment's questions;
- **PR** gets a topic that lets you appear in the media as an expert in your niche.

For PR this is especially important. Journalists and event organisers are not interested in a "full-service company". They are interested in a company that can say something specific about its niche: how purchasing in the industry has changed, why customers stopped buying a certain product, what is happening with prices. Positioning sets that topic.

## Common mistakes

- **Positioning for yourself rather than the customer.** The statement reflects the founder's pride ("we were first", "we are innovative") rather than the reason customers buy.
- **Too broad a segment** out of fear of losing customers.
- **A difference the customer does not care about.** A unique technology the customer never asks about and cannot evaluate.
- **No proof.** A promise without support reads as advertising.
- **Positioning agreed but never applied.** The document sits in a folder while the website, ads and sales scripts stay the same.

## Where to start

Gather your last ten customer conversations — call recordings, chats, meeting notes — and write down the words customers use to describe their problem and why they chose you. That is the raw material for all four parts of the formula.
