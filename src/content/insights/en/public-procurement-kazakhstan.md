Public procurement is a large and stable market for companies that supply goods, carry out works or provide services. Yet many business owners stay out of it, believing the process is too complicated, and many of those who try have bids rejected on formalities. In practice the system is understandable once you know how it works and prepare in advance.

Below: what to know about the current legislation, how to get started on the portal, which procurement methods exist, why bids are rejected and what to prepare before your first submission. This is an orientation overview, not legal advice: each procurement is governed by its own documentation and by the current versions of the law and rules.

## Which law applies

Since 1 January 2025, public procurement in Kazakhstan has been governed by the Law of the Republic of Kazakhstan No. 106-VIII "On Public Procurement" of 1 July 2024, which replaced the previous 2015 law. The procedures are set out in detail in the Rules for Public Procurement approved by the Ministry of Finance. Current texts are published in the Adilet legal information system (adilet.zan.kz).

Among the new features of the law described by court and industry sources:

- **public monitoring** — civil society organisations can monitor planned procurements, including for inflated prices;
- **a rating and scoring system** — when determining the winner, an automated assessment of the supplier's qualifications, financial stability, business reputation and other indicators is taken into account;
- **turnkey construction** — design, construction and commissioning under a single contract;
- **offtake contracts** — a mechanism to support domestic producers.

The law and the rules continue to be refined, so always check the current version before taking part.

## The portal and the digital signature

Public procurement in Kazakhstan is carried out electronically on the **goszakup.gov.kz** web portal. Buyers' procurement plans, announcements, documentation, protocols and results are published there. Bids are submitted through the portal, and contracts are concluded through the portal and certified with the parties' electronic digital signatures.

To get started:

1. **Obtain an electronic digital signature (EDS)** from Kazakhstan's National Certification Authority for the organisation (or sole proprietor) and for the employees who will work on the portal.
2. **Register on the portal** as a potential supplier using the EDS.
3. **Complete the company profile**: registration details, bank details, and information on qualifications, experience, specialists and assets where your procurements require it.
4. **Learn the interface**: searching announcements, submitting bids, security, appeals. The portal has instructions and a help section.

## Procurement methods

The law provides for several procurement methods. The main ones most suppliers encounter:

- **tender (competition)** — for more complex procurements where qualifications and other criteria are assessed alongside price;
- **auction** — the winner is determined by price reduction among the admitted participants;
- **request for price quotations** — for smaller procurements where the main criterion is price;
- **single-source procurement** — in cases expressly provided for by law;
- **through the electronic store** — purchasing goods from an electronic catalogue.

The buyer chooses the method in accordance with the law. For a supplier, it matters to understand each method's specifics: submission deadlines, bid contents, security requirements and how the winner is determined.

## Why bids are rejected

Most rejections have nothing to do with price or product quality; they come down to documents and formal requirements. Typical reasons:

- **An incomplete set of documents** — a required document is missing or unsigned.
- **Expired documents** — licences, certificates, statements.
- **Non-compliance with the technical specification** — the offered goods or services differ from the requirements in characteristics, timing or terms.
- **Errors in qualification information** — experience, specialists or equipment not supported by documents.
- **Bid security problems** where security is required — not provided, provided in the wrong form or the wrong amount.
- **Tax arrears** or being listed in the register of unscrupulous participants.
- **Missed deadlines** — the bid was submitted at the last minute, and a technical glitch or error left no time to fix it.

The good news: almost all of these reasons are removed by preparation and careful checking before submission.

## What to prepare in advance

Before your first bid, collect and put in order:

1. **Constitutional and registration documents** — up to date, in electronic form.
2. **Permits** — licences, certificates and declarations of conformity if your goods or works require them.
3. **Proof of experience** — contracts and certificates of completed work, delivery notes and buyer references in the form in which they are accepted.
4. **Information on specialists** — diplomas, certificates, employment contracts, if procurements require staff qualifications.
5. **Information on equipment and assets** — documents proving ownership or lease.
6. **A clean tax position** — no arrears that could be grounds for rejection.
7. **Templates** — standard forms that repeat from bid to bid, so you do not assemble them from scratch every time.
8. **An owner and a calendar** — one employee responsible for bids and a calendar of deadlines: submission, security, signing, performance.

A prepared document package shortens the time spent on each bid and almost entirely removes the risk of rejection on formalities. Review it once a quarter: documents expire and procurement requirements change.

## How to set up monitoring

Checking the portal occasionally achieves little: procurements are published every day, and submission windows are short. Regular participation starts with a monitoring system.

1. **A search profile.** A list of product, work and service codes, keywords, regions and buyers that suit you. Build it from your past sales and capabilities, not just product names.
2. **Daily screening.** Every day new announcements are checked against the profile, and suitable ones go into a shortlist with the key parameters: buyer, amount, deadline, method, key requirements.
3. **Quick assessment.** For each shortlisted procurement, a "bid / don't bid" decision with a reason. Over time, the reasons for passing show which documents or qualifications you are missing.
4. **Buyers' procurement plans.** Buyers publish annual plans, which let you see in advance what will be purchased and prepare before the announcement.
5. **Results analysis.** Who wins in your procurements, at what price, which bids are rejected and why. Protocols are an open source of market knowledge.

## How to choose which procurements to bid for

Not every procurement whose title fits is worth bidding for. Before submitting, assess:

- **compliance with requirements** — do you have all the required experience, licences and specialists;
- **economics** — does it pay off given the price, payment terms, logistics, security and penalties;
- **deliverability** — can you perform the contract on time and in full;
- **competition** — who usually wins similar procurements from this buyer, and at what price;
- **the buyer's track record** — how they accept work and how promptly they pay.

The last point is often underestimated: a won procurement that cannot be delivered profitably is worse than a lost one. It helps to decide in advance on a minimum margin below which the company does not bid — and not to go below it in the heat of bidding, especially in auctions where the price falls in real time.

## If you disagree with a decision

Actions and decisions of the buyer, the organiser and the commission can be appealed in the manner set by the public procurement law. Appeal deadlines are short, so read the protocols for your bids as soon as they are published. An appeal makes sense when there are specific grounds: a bid was rejected for a reason that does not match the documentation or the law, or the documentation's requirements unreasonably restrict competition. The law separately specifies cases where results cannot be appealed — for example, a winner determined by the rating and scoring system, according to court sources.

## After winning

Winning is the beginning, not the end. You then need to sign the contract on the portal on time, provide performance security where required, fulfil the obligations and document acceptance correctly. Evading the contract or failing to perform it properly can lead to inclusion in the register of unscrupulous public procurement participants, which bars participation for the period set by law. So already at the bidding stage you should know how the contract will be delivered.
