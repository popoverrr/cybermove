For a service business, SEO often looks like a black box: the contractor does something, reports show rankings rising for unknown queries, and enquiries stay the same. Or the opposite: the website brings in nothing for years, and the owner decides that "SEO doesn't work in our niche".

In reality, search optimisation for a service company follows a fairly clear logic. There is work that reliably produces results over several months, and there is work that never will. Let's go through both.

## How customers search for a service

Before talking about SEO, it helps to look at search through the customer's eyes. Someone who needs a service searches differently at different stages:

- **Problem:** "why is my boiler not heating", "how to lower cost per lead".
- **Solution:** "boiler maintenance", "ad account audit".
- **Choosing a provider:** "boiler maintenance Almaty price", "ad audit for business reviews".

Each stage needs its own page. Problem queries are covered by articles and guides; solution and provider queries by service pages. A company with a single "Services" page listing everything cannot answer any of these queries precisely.

## What pays off in six months

### A separate page for every service

What usually makes the most noticeable difference for a service company is a separate page for each service, with a clear URL, a title built around the main query and genuinely useful content: what is included, how the work goes, timelines, the price or how it is calculated, who it suits, examples of work and answers to common questions. The page should answer a customer's questions better than competitors' pages do.

An anchor on a general page ("/services#audit") cannot be shown by a search engine as a separate result. A separate URL can.

### Technical health

The search engine must be able to find, read and correctly understand every page:

- pages are open for indexing, with no accidental blocks in robots.txt or meta tags;
- there is a sitemap listing all important pages;
- every page has one address, with duplicates redirected or a canonical URL set;
- the site runs on HTTPS, loads quickly and works well on phones;
- every page has a unique title and description;
- headings follow a logical order: one H1, then H2 and H3 without skipping levels;
- if the site is multilingual, the language versions are linked with hreflang markup.

This work does not bring traffic by itself, but nothing else works without it. Repeat the technical check after every major site update: new sections, a new template or plugin often quietly break what was already set up.

### Content that answers questions

Articles and guides that answer customers' questions at the problem and selection stages bring people who are not yet looking for a provider but soon will be. Each article should link to the relevant service page. Google's guidance for creators is explicit about prioritising helpful, people-first content — with experience, specifics and a clear author or source — over content made for search engines.

### Local signals

For services delivered in specific cities, these matter:

- a Google Business Profile with the exact name, address, phone, opening hours, categories and photos;
- the same name, address and phone number on the website, the profile and directories;
- mentions of cities and districts on service pages where they are true: real sites, projects, service areas.

Doorway pages for "service + city", with identical text and only the city name changed, are a bad idea: search engines treat them as spam.

### Structured data

Schema.org markup helps the search engine understand what is on a page: the organisation, a service, breadcrumbs, questions and answers, an article. It does not guarantee rich results, but it makes the site easier to understand for search and for the AI assistants that increasingly answer users' questions.

### Links and mentions

Mentions of the company on industry sites, in the media, in partner directories, on client and association websites. What works is what is earned naturally: expert publications, talks, joint projects, case studies published by partners.

## What will never pay off

- **Buying links in bulk** on dubious sites. At best it does nothing; at worst it leads to penalties.
- **Text "for robots"** stuffed with keywords, hidden text, over-optimised headings.
- **Doorway pages** for every city or query variation with the same content.
- **Mass-generated articles without editing**, published for volume. Volume without value does not help and can hurt how the whole site is assessed.
- **Changing contractors every two months.** SEO compounds, and constant restarts with a new strategy wipe out the work before it has time to pay off.
- **Ranking promises.** In its guidance on hiring an SEO, Google states plainly that no one can guarantee a number one ranking.
- **Ranking reports disconnected from enquiries.** Growth for queries nobody searches for, or that never lead to customers, is meaningless.

## Redesigns and migrations: how not to lose what you have

One of the most common causes of a sudden traffic drop is a new website. The company changes the design or platform, page URLs change, and the old pages simply stop existing. Everything the search engine knew about the site has to be rebuilt from scratch.

To avoid it:

- before launch, list every old URL that received traffic or had external links;
- set up a permanent (301) redirect from each old URL to the matching new page, not to the home page;
- carry over the text and headings of pages that performed well instead of replacing them "for looks";
- after launch, update the sitemap and watch Search Console for errors and pages dropping out of the index.

## SEO and AI assistant answers

More and more people ask questions not in a search box but to AI assistants, and see an answer assembled from several sources. For a service company this does not replace SEO — it reinforces its fundamentals: assistants rely on pages that answer the question clearly, are well structured and contain verifiable facts. Separate service pages, specific guides, Schema.org markup and clear company information help in both channels.

## What to ask an SEO contractor

- What work will be done in the first three months, and why that work?
- What data will results be judged on — only rankings, or also enquiries and customers?
- Will new pages be created, and who writes the copy for them?
- How will links and mentions be earned?
- What stays with the company if the work stops: access, texts, reports?

Answers like "we guarantee top 10 in a month" or "our link sources are a secret" are a reason to look for someone else.

## How to measure SEO

Rankings are an intermediate metric. For the business these matter more:

- **impressions and clicks from search** for service pages and articles — Google Search Console data;
- **enquiries from organic traffic** — goals in analytics and the source in the CRM;
- **branded vs non-branded traffic** — whether searches for your services grow, not just searches for your name;
- **cost per customer from SEO** compared with paid channels.

Connect Search Console from day one: it is a free source of data on which queries Google shows your site for and what problems it has.

## The order of work for a service company

1. **Audit and keyword research.** A technical check, demand and competitor analysis, a keyword map matching each query to a page.
2. **Technical fixes.** Indexing, speed, mobile, redirects, meta tags, markup.
3. **Service pages.** A separate, complete page for each service.
4. **Content.** Articles answering customer questions, with internal links to services.
5. **Local signals and mentions.** Business profile, directories, publications, partners.
6. **Measurement and refinement.** Regular review of Search Console and CRM data, improving pages that get impressions but not clicks.
