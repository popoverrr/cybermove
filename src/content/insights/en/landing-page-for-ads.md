Ads bring a person to a page, and from there the page decides everything. You can set up a campaign perfectly, find the right audience and make a strong creative — and still lose most of the paid clicks on the first screen. In the ad account the problem shows up as "expensive advertising", so the budget gets changed in the wrong place.

Let's go through what a landing page for ads should contain, how to check its speed and analytics, and which mistakes come up most often.

## The main rule: the page continues the ad

The person clicked on a specific promise: "aerial platform rental in Almaty with an operator", "heating installation for a warehouse", "ad account audit in a week". On the page they need to see that promise continued immediately — the same words, the same product, the same offer. If they land on a home page listing every service instead, they have to search on their own, and most people do not.

The practical consequence: one campaign or ad group — one landing page built for its search intent. It does not have to be a separate website: it can be a service page on your main site, as long as it answers the query precisely.

## Landing page structure

There is no fixed template, but pages that work share a logic — they answer the visitor's questions in the order they arise.

### First screen: what is this, and why me

- A headline that repeats the essence of the ad and names the result for the customer.
- One or two lines of detail: who it is for, which city, what timeline.
- The main action — a button to enquire, call or message.
- An image of the product or the result, not an abstract "team in the office".

On a phone, the first screen is the single most important area in all of your marketing. Check it on a phone.

### What's included and how it works

Specifics instead of generalities: what the customer gets, what the steps are, what you need from them. For a complex service, a simple diagram of three to five steps helps.

### Proof

Case studies, photos of real projects, client logos (with permission), certificates, guarantees, contract terms. Anything that shows you have done this for others before. Invented testimonials and "happy customer counters" work against you — people see through them.

### Price, or how it is calculated

If you cannot state a price, explain what it depends on and how quickly you will quote. No price information at all sends people off to compare competitors.

### Answers to objections

Timelines, guarantees, payment, delivery, what happens if something goes wrong. A short FAQ block works well.

### The action again

At the end of the page, repeat the main action from the first screen. Someone who has read to the end is ready to enquire and should not have to scroll back up.

## Search and social bring different visitors

The same page performs differently for search and social traffic, because people arrive in a different state of mind.

**From search** (Google Ads, SEO) comes someone who has already formulated the task: "25 ton crane rental Almaty", "boiler cleaning price". They need to confirm quickly that they are in the right place, see the terms and get in touch. Short pages with specifics on the first screen work for them: the exact service name, the city, the price or how it is calculated, a call button.

**From social** (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok) comes someone who was not searching for anything: an ad caught their interest. They are not yet sure they need it. For them explanation matters more: what problem the product solves, what it looks like in real life, why they should trust you. The page is longer, with more visual proof and answers to doubts, and the main action is often softer — send a question in a messenger rather than request a quote.

If both kinds of traffic go to one page, compare their behaviour separately. Often the page works well for search and poorly for social, and what you need is a second version, not new ads.

## Forms and messengers

The form is where more almost-won enquiries are lost than anywhere else.

- **Minimum fields.** Name and phone are usually enough for a first contact. Every extra field reduces the share of people who submit.
- **A clear outcome.** After submitting, the person sees that the request was received and when you will get back to them.
- **Messengers next to the form.** For many customers it is easier to write in WhatsApp than to fill in a form. The messenger button should open a chat with a prepared first message that shows which page the person came from.
- **Delivery checks.** Every enquiry must reliably reach the CRM, or at least email and the messenger of the person responsible. A broken form nobody knows about is one of the most expensive failures in marketing.

## Speed and mobile

Google measures page experience with Core Web Vitals. The thresholds Google publishes for a good experience: largest contentful paint (LCP) within 2.5 seconds, interaction to next paint (INP) within 200 milliseconds, cumulative layout shift (CLS) no more than 0.1. You can check any page for free in PageSpeed Insights.

What slows landing pages down most often:

- heavy, uncompressed images and videos on the first screen;
- dozens of scripts — chats, widgets, pixels nobody uses;
- fonts loaded from several external services;
- site builders that load all their code on every page.

Speed affects both visitor behaviour and advertising: in Google Ads, landing page experience is one of the components of ad quality.

## Analytics: without it the page cannot be improved

A page without analytics is an opinion. The minimum set:

1. **An analytics tag** (for example Google Analytics 4) and the pixels of the ad platforms you use.
2. **Goals for every way to get in touch:** form submission, phone click, messenger click. Count only forms, and messengers and calls "disappear" from the statistics.
3. **Conversions sent back to ad accounts.** Platform algorithms optimise delivery for the actions they know about. If conversions are not passed back, the ads learn from clicks.
4. **UTM tags on every ad**, with the source passed into the CRM with each enquiry. Only then can you calculate cost per customer, not just cost per lead.

Reconcile the data: the number of enquiries in the CRM and the number of conversions in the ad account should roughly match. A large gap means tracking is broken somewhere.

## Common mistakes

- Ads lead to the home page instead of a page built for the query.
- The first screen is taken by a slider, pushing the headline and button below.
- The form asks for email, company and comments before the first contact.
- On a phone, some buttons are covered by a chat widget or a cookie banner.
- Goals track only the form; messengers and calls are not counted.
- The page has not changed since launch, although behavioural data has piled up.

## How to improve the page

A landing page is not a project with a delivery date; it is a tool you refine with data. Good practice is to review, every few weeks, where people leave the page, compare versions of the first screen or offer, and change one meaningful thing at a time. Tests make sense with enough traffic; with few visitors, fix the obvious mistakes from the list above first.

What to look at first:

- **first-screen bounce rate** for each traffic source — shows whether the page matches the expectation set by the ad;
- **scroll depth** — which block most visitors reach and where they stop reading;
- **clicks on buttons and links** — which contact options people actually choose;
- **session recordings** on phones — where people get stuck, what they try to tap, what they miss;
- **lead quality in the CRM** — how many enquiries from this page turn into sales.

The last point matters more than all the others: a page that produces many enquiries but few customers is working worse than the report suggests.
